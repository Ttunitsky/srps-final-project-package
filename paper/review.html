A Certified Primal–Dual Solver for the Selective Routing Problem with Synchronization, and a Falsification Study of Ten Strengthening Techniques

David Chouchena · Tomer Tunitsky Tel-Aviv University August 2026

Abstract

We study the Selective Routing Problem with Synchronization (SRPS), a profit-maximising variant of the Team Orienteering Problem in which each selected job must be served simultaneously by a prescribed subset of processors under a global time limit [1]. Exact branch-and-price-and-cut algorithms solve instances up to n=60 but leave nonzero primal–dual gaps on the hardest class and report no solutions for larger instances.

We present a certified primal–dual solver for SRPS, combining an Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search with a Lagrangian relaxation of the joint-service coupling. A synchronisation-aware insertion procedure evaluates up to 5|Kj| cross-route position combinations per job; a subgradient scheme yields per-instance Lagrangian upper bounds refreshed in-loop via a warm-started Polyak step, producing a valid certificate and its optimality gap for every solution. On the standard 660-instance benchmark it attains a mean certified gap of 0.133%, median 0.046%, maximum 1.965%; 94.1% of instances are certified within 0.5% and 283 (42.9%) are proven optimal. It reproduces the proven optimum on 98.4% of instances the exact method solves and supplies certified solutions for all 415 it fails to close — 196 of them first-ever results.

The second half of the report is a systematic attempt to strengthen that solver using ten techniques from the syllabus, and an account of why nine fail. Two were the primary brief: importing the cut separation logic of the exact method, and coupling the search more tightly to the Lagrangian dual. Both are falsified on structural rather than parametric grounds. Separation against the incumbent can never find a violated cut, because the incumbent is feasible by construction; redirected at the relaxed solution, where violations demonstrably exist, the natural feasibility cut is unsound on the majority of instances tested. The dual-guided operators are degenerate under their own initialisation — the fair profit split forces the reduced profit to be identically zero for every job — and once repaired, an isolating control attributes the entire apparent effect to the warm dual start, which then loses 93% of its magnitude at production budget.

The remaining techniques are unified by a ceiling argument computable in advance from the solver's own results: because the optimum is known only to lie between the incumbent and the bound, any gap-directed technique is capped by the current certified gap — 0.11 percentage points on average and 0.009 at the median, beneath the noise floor of the apparatus built to detect it. Three of the ten are closed analytically rather than experimentally, by the integrality condition of [7], the Dantzig–Wolfe equivalence [13], and the separability forfeit of surrogate relaxation [14]. The single intervention that improves what it targets is parallelising the per-processor dual subproblems, which attacks runtime — the one metric the ceiling does not bound. Three transferable measurement traps are documented along the way.

Introduction

The Selective Routing Problem with Synchronization (SRPS) arises in telescope scheduling at the Gran Telescopio Canarias, where observations require coordinated use of multiple shared resources under a global time budget [1]; scheduling observations on instruments and telescope arrays under resource and time constraints is an active application area for operational-research methods [18]. SRPS asks which jobs to select, and in what processor routes to embed them, so as to maximise collected profit while ensuring that every selected multi-processor job is jointly served and scheduled feasibly.

SRPS belongs to the profit-collecting routing family [19] rooted in the Orienteering Problem [20, 31] and the Team Orienteering Problem [21], but differs from standard TOP by requiring joint service across subsets of processors [3]. This synchronisation requirement creates a coupling between routes that destroys the separability exploited by most classical orienteering heuristics and exact methods.

Riera-Ledesma and Salazar-González [1] introduced SRPS together with an exact branch-and-price-and-cut (BPC) algorithm and a benchmark suite of eight instance families. Their method proves optimality for many instances, but on the hardest class it leaves nonzero primal–dual gaps at n=60 and reports no solutions for larger instances. At the same time, no heuristic or primal–dual approximation method has been proposed specifically for SRPS.

The first half of this report develops a certified primal–dual solver for SRPS. The primal component is an Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search whose main problem-specific ingredient is a synchronisation-aware insertion procedure: inserting a |Kj|=4 job requires evaluating up to 54=625 cross-route position combinations, each checked by a longest-path feasibility oracle. The dual component is a Lagrangian relaxation that yields hard per-instance upper bounds and is re-tightened in-loop, so the method is not merely a heuristic but a complete primal–dual procedure returning both a feasible solution and a certificate.

The second half asks a single question in ten forms: can a technique from the exact-algorithms syllabus tighten that certificate? The techniques are listed in Table 2 and treated one per subsection in Section 9, each with its design, the reason it should have worked, its measured result, and a discussion of what the result means. Nine fail. The failures are structural rather than parametric — none would be repaired by better tuning — and they fall into four kinds: impossible by construction, unsound, real but unusable, and artifacts of measurement.

Section 7 derives the argument that organises the whole second half. Because the optimum P* is known only to lie in [z, ub], any technique acting on either end of that interval is bounded by the interval's current width. That width averages 0.11 percentage points and is 0.009 at the median, which is smaller than the arm-to-arm noise of the experiments built to detect improvements. The bound costs seconds to compute from data already in hand. That we computed it after rather than before the calibration campaigns is itself one of the findings.

The exception proves the rule: the one technique that improves what it targets is parallel evaluation of the per-processor dual subproblems, and it is the one technique aimed at runtime, which the ceiling does not bound.

Problem description

2.1 · Formal SRPS definition and notation

Let J={1,…,n} denote the job set and K the set of processors. Each job j∈J yields profit bj and requires a subset Kj⊆K of processors. For each processor k∈K, let Jk⊆J denote the jobs requiring processor k. Travel and processing times are given by a matrix (tiℓ), and the planning horizon is bounded by a global time limit L.

SRPS seeks a set of elementary processor routes Pk and a selected job set such that each selected job j is served by every processor in Kj, all required processors start service at a common feasible time, and the global time limit is respected. The objective is to maximise total collected profit Σj∈J bj yj.

2.2 · Binding constraints

Before stating the model algebraically we name the three constraints it encodes, because their interaction — particularly the second — is easy to misread.

  1. Horizon. Elapsed time from the start depot to the end depot may not exceed L on any processor.
  2. Synchronisation. All processors in Kj must start job j at the same instant sj. For every arc (i→ℓ) traversed by any processor, sℓ−si ≥ tiℓ. Processors may idle before a synchronised start, and that idle time counts toward L.
  3. Consistency. If any processor serves j, every processor in Kj must serve j. Partial service is forbidden.

Constraints 2 and 3 are distinct requirements and are often conflated: consistency says which processors must serve a job, synchronisation says when they must do so. A solution can satisfy either without the other.

Constraint 2 is a difference-constraint system: earliest start times are given by longest paths in the arc-weighted graph induced by the routes, and a cyclic graph means no feasible schedule exists. This is what makes feasibility checkable in linear time, and it is the check the heuristic of Section 5 uses. The idle-time clause is what separates "route length ≤ L" from "makespan ≤ L": routes that each fit comfortably inside the horizon may still be jointly infeasible once synchronised waiting is charged.

2.3 · Full optimisation model (self-contained)

We adopt the compact arc-flow SRPS-1 structure of [1]. Let Vk=Jk∪{0,n+1} be processor k's node set (start depot 0, end depot n+1). For each k∈K, let zkiℓ∈{0,1} indicate whether processor k traverses arc (i,ℓ) and xj,k∈{0,1} whether processor k serves job j. Let yj∈{0,1} indicate whether job j is selected, and sj,k the service-start time of processor k at job j.

maximise Σj∈J bj yj (1)

Route structure is enforced by standard flow constraints — each processor leaves the start depot once and enters the end depot once,

Σℓ∈Vk\{0} zk0ℓ = 1 , Σi∈Vk\{n+1} zki,n+1 = 1 (2)

with degree balance tying arcs to service,

Σi∈Vk\{j} zkij = xj,k , Σℓ∈Vk\{j} zkjℓ = xj,k ∀ j∈Jk (3)

together with elementarity (no repeated job service on a route). Joint-service coupling is

yj ≤ xj,k ∀ j∈J, k∈Kj (4)

so a profit can be collected only if every required processor serves the job. Timing and synchronisation are enforced by arc-time propagation and equal-start constraints:

sℓ,k ≥ si,k + tiℓ − M(1 − zkiℓ) for each used arc (i,ℓ) (5) sj,k = sj,k′ ∀ k,k′ ∈ Kj (6) sn+1,k − s0,k ≤ L ∀ k∈K (7)

This explicit model resolves the synchronisation semantics: selected multi-processor jobs require both route inclusion by all processors in Kj and simultaneous service start across those processors. Appendix A keeps the bound-validity derivation and the exact form of the relaxed Lagrangian subproblems. Section 9.10 solves this formulation directly with a general-purpose MIP solver as an uncoupled reference.

2.4 · Relation to OP, TOP, and synchronisation variants

SRPS reduces to TOP when |Kj|=1 for every job, so it inherits the prize-collecting routing structure of OP/TOP [3]. The distinguishing feature is synchronisation: a selected job may require several processors to serve it jointly, creating inter-route coupling in both selection and timing.

Beyond SRPS, a large literature studies heuristics and exact methods for related orienteering and synchronised routing problems, including TOP and its time-window variants, clustered/team orienteering, and vehicle routing with synchronisation constraints [22,23]. Representative approaches combine branch-and-price, tabu search, large neighbourhood search, and hybrid metaheuristics, but generally do not provide per-instance optimality certificates for the returned solutions. SRPS inherits the profit-maximising structure of these problems while adding a joint-service coupling that removes the route separability exploited by most existing heuristics.

Close relatives differ in important ways. In clustered orienteering with time windows, teams are typically homogeneous and individual members cover separate tasks inside a cluster rather than jointly serving the same job. In synchronised VRPs, the focus is usually cost minimisation with mandatory service [22,23], whereas SRPS is selective and profit maximising. Closely related prize-collecting routing problems with synchronisation have appeared recently — two-echelon prize-collecting routing with vehicle synchronisation [24] and aquaculture service-vessel routing [25] — but these route vehicle fleets under two-echelon or mandatory-task structure rather than the per-job processor-subset coupling of SRPS. These differences justify treating SRPS as a distinct problem class rather than a routine variant of TOP or synchronised VRP.

3

Existing work and benchmark instances

3.1 · Exact work on SRPS

The SRPS literature is currently anchored by the branch-and-price-and-cut algorithm of Riera-Ledesma and Salazar-González [1], built on the compact arc-flow formulation SRPS-1. Two variants are reported: BPC-SRPS-E, which enhances the pricing step, and BPC-SRPS-N, a node-based alternative. The method is run with a 1 CPU-hour limit and reports a primal–dual gap of 100×(dual−primal)/primal.

Its performance establishes both the strength of the exact baseline and the motivation for a complementary primal–dual heuristic. In the hardest class-4 families, all instances up to n≤55 are solved to optimality, but at n=60 nonzero gaps remain, reaching 1.64% on the Euclidean family at α=0.75. For larger class-4 instances (n≥65), no benchmark solutions are published. These two regimes — hard instances with residual exact gaps, and larger instances with no reported exact result — define the main space in which a certified heuristic can add value.

The cut separation of [1], and the branch-and-price framework it sits in [12], are the direct motivation for the first technique studied in Section 9. Branch-price-and-cut separates violated inequalities against the fractional solution of a linear master problem; whether that machinery transfers to a heuristic which never forms such a solution is precisely what Section 9.1 answers.

3.2 · Broader routing and primal–dual context

The broader routing literature offers two complementary precedents. First, ALNS and related destroy–repair metaheuristics have proved effective across TOP, clustered orienteering, and VRP variants with rich side constraints, because they combine strong primal search with problem-specific insertion logic [2,3]; a substantial body of metaheuristics — tabu search, variable neighbourhood descent, and guided local search among them — has been developed specifically for the team orienteering problem [4, 28, 29, 30], and recent reviews catalogue and rank the destroy and repair operators used across these applications [32, 33, 34]. Second, Lagrangian relaxation and subgradient schemes are classical tools for routing problems in which a small set of complicating constraints can be dualised to obtain decomposable subproblems and instance-specific upper bounds [5,6,7], with a long record in vehicle routing [9,10]; matheuristics interleaving such relaxations with metaheuristic search are an established way to obtain both strong primal solutions and bounds [11]. Step sizes follow Polyak [8].

What is missing in the SRPS setting is the combination of these two traditions. Existing SRPS work is exact and benchmark-defining, but does not provide a scalable heuristic counterpart. Existing TOP and synchronised-routing heuristics offer powerful primal search machinery, and Lagrangian relaxations are widely used to derive strong dual bounds in vehicle routing, but to the best of our knowledge there is no method that embeds an ALNS inside an in-loop Lagrangian certification scheme providing per-instance optimality gaps for SRPS. The method described here is designed to supply exactly that combination.

4

Lagrangian relaxation and dual bounding

4.1 · Relaxation formulation

The complicating constraints are the joint-service couplings (4), yj ≤ xj,k, which require that a job earns profit only if every required processor serves it. Relaxing them with multipliers μj,k ≥ 0 yields the Lagrangian function

L(μ) = Σj∈J max( 0, bj − Σk∈Kj μj,k ) + Σk∈K Ok(μ) (8)

where Ok(μ) is the optimum of a single-processor orienteering problem in which job j∈Jk carries the modified profit μj,k; Appendix A states the subproblem precisely and proves the bound. Each Ok(μ) is solved exactly — with no pruning, sampling, or candidate-set restriction — by bitmask dynamic programming in O(2|Jk||Jk|2). The exponential factor is bounded by a small constant rather than by n: because every job requires only |Kj|∈{2,3,4} of the |K|=55 processors, jobs spread thinly and each processor serves only a handful.

Two properties of Ok(μ) matter for interpreting every certificate that follows. First, each subproblem is solved to optimality, so no looseness in the bound originates there, and validity does not depend on how far the subgradient has converged: a short dual run yields a loose certificate, never an invalid one. Second, the subproblem's time matrix charges the processing time of a job plus the travel to the next, and enforces route length ≤ L; it is therefore structurally blind to the synchronised idle time of constraint 2, because each processor is solved in isolation. The relaxed solution is optimistic in that specific, identifiable way, and it is the principal source of the residual looseness reported in Section 6 — a fact Section 9.1 exploits and Section 9.6 returns to.

Proposition 1 — Validity of the Lagrangian bound

Let z*SRPS denote the optimal SRPS objective value, and let L(μ) be the Lagrangian value obtained by relaxing the joint-service constraints with multipliers μ ≥ 0. Then z*SRPS ≤ L(μ) for every μ ≥ 0. Consequently infμ≥0 L(μ) is a valid upper bound on the optimum.

Proof

Any feasible SRPS solution satisfies yj ≤ xj,k for all required processors. After relaxation the nonnegative penalty terms vanish on every feasible SRPS solution, so its objective value cannot exceed the relaxed objective at the same μ. Maximising over feasible SRPS solutions gives z*SRPS ≤ L(μ); taking the infimum over μ ≥ 0 preserves the inequality. Appendix A gives the argument in full. ∎

4.2 · Subgradient scheme

Multipliers are initialised by a fair profit split μj,k = bj/|Kj|, which makes the initial relaxed objective coincide with an independent-orienteering-style bound, so descent can only reduce it. The dual is then tightened by projected subgradient descent with Polyak-style step sizes [8],

ηt = ( L(μt) − θ* ) / ‖gt‖² , gj,k = x*j,k − y*j , μj,k ← max( 0, μj,k − ηt gj,k ) (9)

clamped for stability, with a diminishing fallback schedule when progress stalls. The lower reference θ* is the current incumbent z* throughout — initially the greedy solution, then the refreshed incumbent during in-loop re-bounding. The method uses no published best-known value. The best bound found over the iteration budget is returned together with the corresponding multiplier vector, which is retained as a warm start for the next invocation.

That the fair split is the natural starting point for descent, yet the one multiplier vector at which reduced profits vanish identically, is the whole of the defect analysed in Section 9.2.

4.3 · Certification logic

The algorithm proceeds in four stages on each instance: compute an initial upper bound from decomposed routing relaxations; run adaptive ALNS phases to improve the primal incumbent z*; whenever the primal search stalls, invoke a warm-started Lagrangian re-bound using θ*=z*; and track the certified gap

cert_gap = (UBlag − z*) / UBlag × 100 , UBlag = ⌊ mint L(μt) ⌋ (10)

stopping when the upper bound matches the incumbent, the certified gap falls below 0.3%, or the escalation tiers are exhausted.

The floor is not cosmetic. It is applied before the gap is computed and is valid because all profits bj are integral, so no feasible objective value lies strictly between ⌊L(μ)⌋ and L(μ). A dual improvement smaller than one profit unit therefore leaves the certified gap unchanged: the certificate advances in integer steps even when the underlying real-valued bound improves continuously. Section 9.4 is decided entirely by this.

The method is fully independent of any published benchmark value: both the ALNS and the Lagrangian dual reference use only the current incumbent. A certified gap of x% means the returned solution is guaranteed to lie within x% of the true optimum. A certified gap of 0% means the incumbent equals a valid Lagrangian upper bound; by weak duality this proves the incumbent optimal, and we call such instances proven optimal.

Equation (10) has two levers, and the distinction organises the second half of this report. A mechanism can improve the gap by raising z* (primal) or by lowering UBlag (dual). Cutting planes act on the dual. Neighbourhood operators act on the primal. Section 9 is ordered on exactly that line, and Section 7 shows both levers to be bounded by the same quantity.

4.4 · In-loop warm re-bounding

A single pre-search subgradient run can leave certificates loose on the hardest instances, especially at tight budgets and larger n. The adaptive loop therefore refreshes the dual bound inside the search whenever a phase fails to improve the incumbent. Each re-bound is capped at 200 subgradient iterations or 60 seconds, is warm-started from the multiplier vector attaining the best bound so far, and uses the current incumbent as the Polyak target.

This coupling matters because once the primal search has already found a strong incumbent, the quantity L(μ)−z* approximates the true residual optimality gap more closely than a cold lower reference does. Step sizes become more informative, the dual converges faster toward a useful certificate, and the updated bound directly governs whether further search is worthwhile. Empirically this mechanism is particularly important on the hard tail of the benchmark, where a single pre-search bound leaves the loosest certificates.

4.5 · Tight coupling between ALNS and Lagrangian bounding

The Lagrangian relaxation is not a one-off preprocessing device but a dual mechanism continuously tightened around the current incumbent produced by the ALNS. The key idea is to use the incumbent objective z* as the level reference θ* in the Polyak-style subgradient step, so the dual bound is driven toward the best feasible solution found so far.

This links dual progress directly to the best known feasible solution. When L(μt) is much larger than z*, the Polyak numerator is large and the step is aggressive, reducing the bound quickly; as L(μt) approaches z*, the numerator shrinks, the step decreases, and the updates switch from large corrective moves to fine adjustments around the incumbent level. The shrinkage is not from the numerator alone: the step is clamped, and after a run of non-improving iterations the scheme falls back to a diminishing schedule (Section 4.2). Both devices are already part of the baseline, which matters when reading any comparison against an explicitly stabilised variant — Section 9.5.

The dual update is embedded in the outer adaptive loop. Each ALNS phase attempts to improve z*; whenever a phase fails, an in-loop re-bound is triggered, warm-started from the multipliers that attained the best bound so far and using the current incumbent as the reference. Because each refresh begins at the recorded best multipliers, its first iterate re-evaluates L at exactly the previous best value, so the sequence of bounds across refreshes is monotone non-increasing by construction and no separate guard is required.

The effect is that ALNS and the Lagrangian bound chase each other: ALNS pushes the incumbent upward, while the subgradient scheme pulls L(μ) downward toward that same incumbent. As the incumbent improves, the target in the dual updates rises, forcing the bound to tighten around the best solution available; as the bound approaches the incumbent, step sizes shrink and the bound stabilises.

The coupling is nonetheless narrow in both directions, and stating where it stops matters as much as stating where it acts. The incumbent reaches the dual only through the scalar level reference θ*: its selection structure is never communicated, and multipliers are re-initialised from the previous dual solve rather than from the primal solution. Symmetrically, the dual returns only a bound to the search; the multipliers themselves never enter operator selection, insertion scoring, or the destroy criterion. Both channels are deliberately absent in the configuration evaluated here — and widening either one is exactly what techniques 2 and 4 attempt.

5

Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search

5.1 · Representation and feasibility

A solution consists of a selected job set and one elementary route per processor. Feasibility is maintained by a longest-path schedule check over the induced precedence graph, which verifies that all route arcs and synchronisation precedences can be satisfied within the global time limit. This single check covers constraints 1 and 2 together, including synchronised idle time.

5.2 · Destroy operators

Three destroy operators are used: random destroy, worst destroy, and Shaw destroy. The random operator removes a uniformly sampled subset of selected jobs; worst destroy removes the currently least valuable selected jobs; Shaw destroy removes jobs related to a random pivot under travel-based proximity. The destroy size is sampled dynamically, with escalation tiers enlarging the removed fraction on hard instances.

5.3 · Repair operators and synchronisation-aware insertion

Three repair operators are used: profit-greedy repair, ratio repair, and regret-2 repair. For a candidate job j, the insertion procedure enumerates the top few local positions on each required processor route, forms their Cartesian product, and checks each cross-route placement for feasibility. With the five candidates per processor retained by the implementation, class-4 jobs yield at most 5|Kj| = 625 combinations. Algorithm 1 states the procedure; the three repair operators differ only in the order in which candidate jobs are offered to it.

Proposition 2 — Insertion-complexity bound

Suppose at most c local candidate positions are retained per required processor for job j. Then the synchronisation-aware insertion procedure evaluates at most c|Kj| route-position combinations for that job, each followed by one feasibility check.

Proof

The candidate positions are chosen independently on each processor in Kj (Algorithm 1, lines 4–9). Their Cartesian product therefore contains at most ∏k∈Kj c = c|Kj| combinations. The algorithm checks each candidate combination once at line 13. ∎

This combinatorial insertion is the main source of SRPS-specific content within the ALNS. It is precisely where the synchronisation structure enters the heuristic; the rest of the machinery follows a standard destroy–repair pattern.

5.4 · Acceptance and operator adaptation

The search uses simulated-annealing acceptance, adaptive roulette-wheel operator selection, and exponentially smoothed score updates. Worsening moves may be accepted to escape local minima, while operators that generate improving or accepted moves receive larger future weight.

5.5 · Multi-start execution

The search cycles over six fixed random seeds. Rather than assigning a fixed total iteration budget to every instance, the algorithm allocates runtime through adaptive phases and resets the destroy tier whenever a phase improves the incumbent.

5.6 · Adaptive control loop

The destroy–repair search is embedded in an outer adaptive loop that allocates more effort only where certification remains difficult. Each phase runs the ALNS with cycling seeds for up to 300 seconds. If the phase improves the incumbent, the destroy tier is reset; otherwise the method performs an in-loop Lagrangian re-bound and escalates the destroy fraction from 25% to 33% to 40%.

The primal–dual coupling occurs at the boundary between the incumbent update and the Lagrangian re-bound: the incumbent produced by ALNS becomes the lower reference of the warm-started dual update, and the tightened upper bound then determines whether further search is warranted. The three black-box procedures are INITIAL_UPPER_BOUND (the minimum of an independent-orienteering and a group-decomposition relaxation, both solved by bitmask DP), ALNS_PHASE (one destroy–repair–accept loop with synchronisation-aware insertion and adaptive operator weights), and LAGRANGIAN_REBOUND (the warm-started subgradient scheme of Section 4).

9.1 · Technique 1 — BPC-style cut separation dual

9.1.1 · First design and its structural impossibility

The initial implementation maintained a pool of cuts of three types — circuit, route-length, and synchronisation-path — separated at phase boundaries against the ALNS incumbent, applied through a hard feasibility filter and a soft scoring penalty. The design follows the separation logic of the exact BPC algorithm [1] and the branch-and-price framework it sits in [12].

This design cannot work, for a reason requiring no experiment. Separation is performed against the incumbent, and the incumbent is feasible by construction. Feasibility means precisely: no cycles in the constraint graph, no route over L, and a synchronisation path within L (Section 2.1). These are exactly the three conditions the separator tests. The candidate set is therefore empty at every call, identically, on every instance.

A 29-run calibration suite was nevertheless executed on Stratified-30, consuming 127.9 minutes and tuning eight parameters. Its outcome was a tautology: the recommended configuration was the arm with all cut machinery disabled, and the mean objective delta was identical (−3.300) across the top five arms. Every run reported cuts_added_total = 0.

Mechanism inertness. The machinery activates and reports activation while remaining incapable of influencing the outcome variable. It is more dangerous than an obvious crash, because it produces clean-looking null results that invite the interpretation "the idea does not help" when the correct interpretation is "the idea was never tested".

A telemetry defect compounded it. A counter intended to record penalty applications was incremented once per job removal regardless of any active cut, reporting nonzero values on runs with an empty pool. An analyst reading only the summary columns would have seen a mechanism apparently doing work.

9.1.3 · Redirecting separation at the relaxed solution

The structural argument also indicates the repair. In branch-price-and-cut, separation runs against a fractional solution, which is where violations live [12]. The heuristic has no such solution, but it does have the Lagrangian relaxed solution, and that object is infeasible in a well-defined way: gj,k is nonzero until convergence, and each Ok(μ) ignores synchronisation idle time (Section 4.1).

We built a read-only instrument (S0) that runs the subgradient to convergence, replays the relaxation at μ*, and measures two channels:

  • Channel A (consistency). Jobs partially claimed — served by some but not all processors in Kj, violating constraint 3 directly.
  • Channel B (synchronisation time). The joint difference-constraint system over the relaxed routes: whether it is cyclic, and if not, whether its longest path exceeds L.

Both fire abundantly. Notably, the same cycle-detection routine that returned zero on every one of the 29 calibration runs returns nonzero here without modification. The cut family was never wrong; the object it was pointed at was.

9.1.4 · Why the natural cut is unsound

Channel B appears to be the opportunity: structural infeasibility the decomposition cannot see. It does not survive scrutiny.

The relaxation fixes which jobs each processor takes, not the order. Any single global total order induces per-processor sequences whose arcs all point forward, hence an acyclic graph. Acyclicity is therefore always achievable, and the cycles S0 reports are an artifact of each processor adopting its own locally optimal min-time order. The real question is whether some ordering also satisfies makespan ≤ L.

This is a soundness requirement, not a technicality. A no-good cut asserting "this relaxed selection is infeasible" is valid only if no ordering works. If a feasible ordering exists and the cut is applied anyway, a feasible point is excluded, ub becomes invalid, and every certified gap computed downstream is wrong. For a method whose contribution is certification, that failure mode is worse than adding no cuts at all.

Instrument S0b therefore searches for any global ordering achieving makespan ≤ L by time-boxed random-restart hill climbing, reporting schedulable when one is found. A schedulable verdict is constructive: an explicit feasible ordering is exhibited, so the selection provably cannot be cut.

The margins are narrow: the tightest feasible schedule clears the horizon by 1 time unit, and 12 of the 16 schedulable instances clear it by less than 50.

That narrowness compounds the problem. A separation routine would have to distinguish "feasible by a handful of time units" from "infeasible", and in a certifying method a misclassification does not produce a weak cut: it produces an invalid upper bound and a silently wrong certified gap. The standard discipline for user cuts is explicit on this point — a valid inequality must strengthen the relaxation without excluding feasible integer points [17, 12] — and this cut family cannot meet it.

9.1.5 · Verdict

We falsify cut separation on three independent grounds. First, separation against the incumbent cannot produce a violated cut, confirmed operationally by a 29-run suite reporting no cuts in any run. Second, forcing activation admitted up to fifteen cuts while leaving mean post-evaluation violation at 0.0 and every final objective bit-identical: the cuts were inert rather than ineffective, and an experiment with no power cannot support a claim about efficacy. Third, redirecting separation at the relaxed solution yields a cut family that is unsound on 16 of 30 instances. Independently of design, Table 6 caps any cut family at 0.1136 pp mean and 0.0087 pp median.

Three limits should be stated. We do not claim BPC cuts are ineffective for SRPS in general: we tested three families inside one architecture. The 14 of 30 instances on which no feasible ordering was found remain formally open, since the schedulability search is heuristic; the soundness argument rests only on the 16 constructive cases, which suffices, because a single wrongly excluded feasible point invalidates the bound. And of the three limbs, only the ceiling generalises beyond the tested subsets.

9.1.6 · Can the incumbent itself generate cuts?

A natural question, worth answering explicitly because it motivates much of the design above: the solver always holds a best known solution, so is there information in it that can be used to generate cuts?

The answer is no, and the reason is §9.1.1 stated in general form. A cut is useful only if some point violates it. The incumbent is feasible, so it violates no valid inequality — that is what feasibility means. Any separation routine run against it returns an empty candidate set, whatever the cut family, independently of the particular families we implemented.

What the incumbent can supply is a bound, not a cut. It is a lower bound on the optimum, and the solver already exploits that: z is the Polyak level reference (Section 4.2), the threshold in the fixing tests of §9.9, and one end of the interval producing the ceiling of Table 6. The distinction is between a feasible point, which bounds, and an infeasible one, which cuts. Generating cuts requires an infeasible object, and in this architecture the only one available is the Lagrangian relaxed solution — which is why §9.1.3 redirects separation there, and §9.1.4 shows why even that fails.

9.2 · Technique 2 — Dual-guided destroy and repair primal

9.2.1 · Design and the initialisation degeneracy

Two operators were added to the ALNS portfolio. A guided destroy removes jobs ranked by ascending reduced profit rj, breaking ties on descending dual pressure. A guided repair inserts jobs by a score derived from rj and the insertion cost. Multipliers are optionally refreshed from the in-loop re-bound. This is the primal-side counterpart of the coupling channel Section 4.5 records as deliberately absent.

Multipliers are initialised by the fair profit split (Section 4.2). Then

Σk∈Kj μj,k = |Kj| · bj/|Kj| = bj ⟹ rj = bj − Σk∈Kj μj,k = 0

for every job, identically. Direct measurement confirms it: on one 80-job instance, 0 of 80 jobs have nonzero reduced profit; on a 150-job instance, 0 of 150.

The fair split is the correct starting point for descent, but it is precisely the multiplier vector at which the guidance signal vanishes. Two consequences follow mechanically:

  • Destroy degenerates to random removal. The sort key is (0 + uniform(−10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁶), −dual pressure). The first component is pure noise on an identically zero base, and exact ties have measure zero, so the intended second criterion never breaks anything.
  • Repair degenerates to insertion order. With rj=0 every job takes a constant fallback score, the strict improvement test fails for every candidate after the first, and insertion follows the iteration order of the unserved set.

Both degenerate operators are inserted at position 0 of their portfolios, displacing tuned operators.

9.6–9.8 · Techniques 6–8 — Alternative ways to combine the subproblem solutions

Equation (8) combines the per-processor optima by plain summation: L(μ) is the y-term plus Σk Ok(μ). That is the only aggregation the solver performs, and it is worth asking what alternatives exist and what they would buy. Three are available, and all three can be settled without an experiment. We present them together because they share a single conclusion.

9.6 · Technique 6 — Lagrangian decomposition: why the natural split cannot help

One cell of the design space calls for Lagrangian decomposition rather than the Lagrangian relaxation the solver implements: duplicate the shared variables, assign one copy to each block, and price their disagreement. We did not run it, because for the natural split it can be shown not to help.

Write the problem as max{ cᵀv : v ∈ XA ∩ XB }, where XA collects the per-processor routing and horizon constraints and XB the joint-service coupling xj,k = yj, both intersected with integrality. The solver dualises XB and solves over XA exactly, so its bound is

zLR = max{ cᵀv : v ∈ conv(XA), xj,k = yj }

Lagrangian decomposition instead yields

zLD = max{ cᵀv : v ∈ conv(XA) ∩ conv(XB) }

By the Guignard–Kim result [16, 7], zLD ≤ zLR always, with strict improvement exactly when conv(XB) is strictly contained in the linear description of XB — that is, when the dualised block lacks the integrality property [15].

Here it does not lack it. XB is the set of binary vectors satisfying xj,k = yj: equalities tying each xj,k to a single binary yj. Fixing y determines x, and the remaining polytope is a product of {0,1}-valued coordinates, so every vertex of the linear description has yj ∈ {0,1}. Hence conv(XB) coincides with that description, and

zLD = zLR

Duplicating variables for the coupling constraint is guaranteed to reproduce the bound the solver already computes. No experiment is required, and none would be informative: the two bounds are equal by construction, so any measured difference could only be numerical noise.

The split that could help. A different partition is not covered by this argument. Separating routing from synchronisation timing — one block enforcing per-processor routes, the other the difference-constraint system on start times — dualises a copy constraint whose blocks both have non-integral hulls, so zLD < zLR becomes possible. This is also the split targeting a deficiency we measured rather than a hypothetical one: the per-processor subproblem enforces route length but is blind to synchronisation waiting (Sections 2.1, 4.1). We record it as future work. It requires a second subproblem family, a timing oracle, and a second multiplier vector, and its payoff remains capped by Table 6. It is the only remaining idea in this study with a mechanism pointing at a measured source of looseness, which is why we name it precisely rather than leaving the cell blank.

9.7 · Technique 7 — Column generation and Dantzig–Wolfe reformulation

Reformulate with one column per per-processor route and price columns by the same orienteering subproblem — the natural next step, and the one the exact literature takes [1, 12]. By the Dantzig–Wolfe equivalence [13], the linear master's bound at convergence equals the Lagrangian dual optimum minμ L(μ) for the same subproblem structure. Column generation therefore cannot yield a tighter bound than a fully converged subgradient; it can only reach it differently.

That is not nothing — an LP master does not oscillate, and §9.5 shows the subgradient still descending when its budget expires, which is precisely the regime where a master would help. But the improvement on offer is one of convergence, not of bound quality, and it is bounded by the same ceiling. It is also expensive: a restricted master, a pricing loop, and stabilisation [26] to make it behave, for a bound the existing scheme reaches asymptotically.

9.8 · Technique 8 — Surrogate relaxation

Aggregate the coupling constraints into a single weighted surrogate rather than dualising each [14]. The surrogate bound dominates the Lagrangian bound in theory — this is the one alternative that could in principle be strictly tighter. But the surrogate subproblem retains the aggregated constraint and is no longer separable by processor, forfeiting precisely the property that makes Ok(μ) exactly solvable by bitmask DP in O(2|Jk||Jk|²). Without separability the subproblem is over all 55 processors jointly, and the exactness on which every certificate in Section 6 depends is lost.

The aggregation that would tighten the bound is the one that destroys the decomposition.

The pattern across techniques 6–8 matches the ceiling result. The aggregation is not where the looseness lives. The looseness lives in the subproblem's blindness to synchronisation waiting (Section 4.1), and no way of combining blind subproblem solutions restores what the blindness discarded.

9.9 · Technique 9 — Reduced-cost variable fixing runtime

Runtime carries no ceiling. Both remaining techniques were selected for that reason.

Lagrangian reduced-cost fixing is the one technique here whose target is runtime rather than gap. Writing G = L(μ) − z, forcing a single yj shifts the bound by one term only:

L(μ | yj=1) = L(μ) + min(0, rj), L(μ | yj=0) = L(μ) − max(0, rj)

so rj < −G proves j absent from every optimal solution and rj > G proves it present. Both tests are O(1) per job given rj, which the dual already computes. The device is classical [5, 17] and, unlike everything in §9.1, unimpeachably sound.

Methodological lessons

Four of these are transferable beyond SRPS, and each was learned by walking into the corresponding trap.

Activation is not effect

A mechanism can fire, report firing, and still be incapable of changing the outcome. The activation sweep of Table 10 passed an explicit activation gate at ε = −100 while remaining completely inert. Gates must therefore be placed on the outcome variable: before spending a calibration suite, assert on a small subset that the treated arm differs from control. That guard would have caught the failure of §9.1 in roughly fifteen minutes instead of 128.

Budgets decide verdicts, in both directions

Reduced-budget screening is standard practice, and it produced here a confident, internally consistent, entirely spurious 0.13 pp result. Nothing inside the screen betrays it: arm ordering is stable and the effect sits far outside run-to-run noise. The failure mode is specific — a starved baseline flatters any treatment that supplies the starved resource.

The same mechanism runs the other way, and we walked into it. The trust region measured −8.07 at 60 iterations, +1.58 at 200, and led on every instance at 1000. We declared it falsified on the middle number. It was not falsified; the arms had not yet separated. A negative verdict taken at a single budget is as unsafe as a positive one. Three of this study's findings — the fast screen, the trust region, and reduced-cost fixing — turn on this, which suggests it is the dominant confound in metaheuristic calibration rather than an incidental one.

Check the ceiling before calibrating

Table 6 costs seconds and uses only data already in hand. It establishes that the median instance offers 0.0087 pp of headroom — below experimental noise — which is decisive information about whether a calibration campaign can possibly succeed. It should precede the campaign, not follow it.

Verify that the signal is non-degenerate

The defect of §9.2.1 is a single arithmetic identity, Σk bj/|Kj| = bj, checkable in one line before any experiment. Guidance mechanisms should assert that their signal has nonzero variance across the decision set at the point of use.

Distinguish soundness failures from performance failures

The result of §9.1.4 is not that the cut performs poorly, but that it is invalid on a majority of instances. In a certifying method, an invalid cut does not degrade the result — it corrupts the guarantee, silently. Soundness must be established before efficacy is measured.

12

Threats to validity

The schedulability search of §9.1.4 is heuristic. A schedulable verdict is constructive and therefore sound: an explicit feasible ordering is exhibited. A "none found" verdict is not a proof of infeasibility, and we do not treat it as one; those instances remain formally open, and settling them would require an exact feasibility oracle over the disjunctive orderings, which is NP-hard in general.

The S0 and S0b samples are subsets of the benchmark rather than its entirety, selected to include both the stratified subset and the loosest-gap instances. The ceiling argument carries no such limitation, being computed over 780 instances, and it is the argument on which the principal conclusion rests.

The corrected dual-guided arms carry a runtime confound by construction: warm initialisation spends an additional subgradient solve the plain control does not pay. We control for it with a dedicated arm applying warm initialisation with both guided operators disabled. A second confound, the reduced screening budget, is not merely controlled but measured: §9.3 re-runs the decisive arms at full budget.

All results are measured against the solver configured as in Section 5.6, in particular a dual refresh capped at 200 iterations or 60 seconds. §9.5 shows that budget to be the binding constraint on the dual: the subgradient is still descending when it stops. Raising it tightens the certificate on every hard-tail instance tested, by 0.29 pp on average at essentially no runtime cost. Conclusions about gap-directed techniques become more secure under such a configuration, since a tighter ub shrinks the ceiling and leaves any technique less room; conclusions that are themselves budget-conditional — the trust region above all — would need re-testing.

The runtime comparisons of Section 6.1 are cross-hardware and cross-language, and we make no normalised speed claim from them; the caveat is stated in full there.

One cell of the design space is named and left empty. Benders decomposition [27] is not covered by any argument above. Fixing the selection y and the per-processor routes leaves the synchronisation feasibility question as a difference-constraint system — a pure LP, and exactly the structure Benders partitioning is designed for, with feasibility cuts generated from its dual rays. It targets the one deficiency this study measured rather than assumed: the subproblem's blindness to synchronised idle time (Sections 4.1, 9.1.3). We did not test it, for the same reason we report it: it requires a master over selection and routing that the ALNS does not expose, and its payoff is capped by Table 6 like every other dual-side device. Together with the routing/timing split of §9.6 it is the second of exactly two remaining directions with a mechanism pointing at a measured source of looseness.

Finally, we tested three specific cut families and one dual-guidance design. We do not claim that no cut or coupling mechanism can help SRPS heuristics in general. We claim that these do not, that the reasons are structural, and that the ceiling bounds what any successor could achieve on this benchmark.

Discussion

The computational evidence supports a complementary role relative to exact BPC, not a replacement claim. On instances where BPC proves optimality quickly, the present method mainly re-validates known optima; where BPC reaches its time limit with residual gaps or no reported solution, it contributes certified near-optimal incumbents. A practical workflow is therefore sequential: run the exact method first, then apply the certified heuristic on unresolved instances.

The objective comparisons and the bound comparisons should be interpreted separately. BPC's published primal–dual gaps and the present certified gaps are derived from different relaxations and different denominators, so they are not numerically identical metrics. The meaningful cross-method comparison is coverage and incumbent quality under certification, not a one-to-one gap-value equivalence.

The improved incumbents reported here should be read as benchmark updates, not as claims that the exact method is dominated. An improved published incumbent indicates that the recorded best-known value can be tightened, while the accompanying certificate quantifies proximity to optimality. This is especially relevant on the open class-4 frontier, where benchmark coverage rather than formal dominance is the main scientific value.

On the second half of the study, the honest reading is that the solver was already close enough to its own bound that the syllabus's strengthening toolkit had nothing left to work with, and that this was knowable in advance from data the solver itself had produced. That is not a negative result about the techniques. Cut separation, dual stabilisation, column generation, and reduced-cost fixing are all effective in the settings they were designed for — inside exact branch-and-bound frameworks, where the relaxation is fractional, the bound is weak, and the search tree is large. The mismatch is architectural: a certified heuristic holds a feasible incumbent rather than a fractional one, computes its bound from an exactly-solved subproblem rather than an LP, and has no tree to prune. Each of those three properties disables a different technique, and §9.1.6 makes the first of them precise.

What remains is the diagnostic contribution. Two of the three measurement traps documented here — mechanism inertness and budget-conditional verdicts — produced results that were internally consistent, plausibly signed, and wrong. Neither would have been caught by more replication, more instances, or tighter statistics, because neither is a variance problem. They are identification problems, and the guards against them are structural: gate on the outcome, and re-test every verdict at the budget where it will be used.

14

Conclusions

This report presents a certified primal–dual method for SRPS, combining a synchronisation-aware ALNS with a Lagrangian relaxation that provides valid per-instance upper bounds, and then documents ten attempts to strengthen it with techniques from the exact-algorithms syllabus. Nine attempts failed, and the reasons are structural rather than parametric.

On the solver itself: the method is best understood as a complement to exact BPC. It reproduces exact results closely where they are known — recovering 241 of 245 BPC-proven optima — and extends the benchmark with certified near-optimal solutions where the exact method leaves gaps or no published result, supplying 196 first-ever certified results and improving 24 published incumbents. Mean certified gap is 0.133% over 660 instances, with 42.9% proven optimal and a median runtime of 5.1 minutes.

Cut separation fails three times over. Separation against a feasible incumbent is mathematically incapable of finding a violated cut. Redirected at the Lagrangian relaxed solution it finds violations in abundance, but the natural feasibility cut is unsound on the majority of instances tested, because the relaxed selection admits a feasible synchronised schedule once ordering is chosen globally rather than per processor. And independently of design, the certified bound is already exact on 48.5% of instances, capping every cut family at 0.11 pp mean and 0.009 pp median improvement.

Dual-guided coupling fails twice over. The operator design is degenerate under its own initialisation, and correcting that defect was worth doing because it converts an untested hypothesis into a tested one. The corrected matrix then appears positive, and is not: an isolating control attributes the movement to the warm-start dual solve rather than to guidance, and a full-budget re-run shows that residue to be a screening artifact as well. Guidance measured against a properly-fed control is +0.0037 pp — on the wrong side of zero.

Of the remaining techniques, Lagrangian decomposition of the natural split is provably equivalent to the relaxation already in use; the Dantzig–Wolfe master converges to the same dual optimum; surrogate relaxation would tighten the bound only by destroying the separability the bound depends on; seeding and stabilisation fail to move the floored bound; and reduced-cost fixing, though sound and cheap, fires only once the incumbent is already essentially optimal, at which point no search remains to accelerate. The expected asymmetry — that the tightness capping cut-based strengthening should make the fixing threshold easy to clear — does not hold, because both properties are consequences of the same quantity G and cannot be played against each other.

Parallelising the per-processor subproblems is the one intervention that improves what it targets: 1.42× on the dual with bit-identical bounds, capturing 59% of a ceiling itself limited because a single subproblem can carry half an instance's work. Its end-to-end value is nonetheless about one per cent, because the dual is invoked on one instance in twelve. A correct optimisation of something that is not the bottleneck is still not a contribution to the solver, and we report it as such.

The broader contribution is diagnostic. Mechanism inertness — machinery that activates, reports activation, and cannot affect the outcome — is a failure mode producing clean, publishable-looking null results from experiments that never took place. The guard against it is cheap and general: gate on the outcome variable, never on whether the mechanism fired. And no verdict, positive or negative, should be believed at a single computational budget.

Two directions remain open with a mechanism pointing at a measured source of looseness rather than a hypothetical one, and both attack the same deficiency — the subproblem's blindness to synchronised idle time. The first is a Lagrangian decomposition splitting routing from synchronisation timing, where both blocks have non-integral hulls and strict improvement is therefore possible (§9.6). The second is Benders decomposition over the difference-constraint system that decides synchronisation feasibility (Section 12). Both remain capped by the ceiling of Table 6, which is the honest summary of what is left to win.

Appendix A

SRPS model, Lagrangian relaxation, and bound validity

This appendix gives the formal basis for the certified gap: the model we relax, the relaxed problem and its per-processor decomposition, the exact subproblem Ok(μ), and a proof that UBlag is a valid upper bound.

A.1 · SRPS model

We adopt the arc-flow formulation SRPS-1 of Riera-Ledesma and Salazar-González [1] and restate only the structure needed below. For each processor k∈K a set of binary arc variables defines an elementary route Pk that starts and ends at the depot; xj,k∈{0,1} records whether Pk serves job j∈Jk, and yj∈{0,1} records whether job j is collected. A solution (y,{Pk}) is SRPS-feasible if

  1. each Pk is an elementary route over Jk respecting travel and processing times (route continuity and elementarity);
  2. the longest-path schedule of the served jobs admits, for every collected multi-processor job j, a common start time across all processors in Kj and respects the global horizon L (synchronisation and time budget); and
  3. yj ≤ xj,k for all j∈J, k∈Kj (joint service).

Writing 𝒳 for the set of SRPS-feasible solutions, z*SRPS = max(y,{Pk})∈𝒳 Σj∈J bj yj.

A.2 · Lagrangian relaxation of the joint-service coupling

We dualise the coupling constraints (iii) with multipliers μj,k ≥ 0 and, in the resulting subproblem, treat each processor route independently subject to its own time budget (the inter-processor synchronisation (ii) is relaxed). For μ ≥ 0,

L(μ) = max(y,{Pk}) Σj∈J bj yj + Σj∈J Σk∈Kj μj,k ( xj,k − yj )

the maximisation being over y∈{0,1}n and independent per-processor elementary routes within horizon L. Regrouping,

L(μ) = Σj∈J max( 0, bj − Σk∈Kj μj,k ) + Σk∈K Ok(μ)

where the first term follows because each yj is now unconstrained and set to 1 iff its reduced profit is positive, and

Ok(μ) = max{ Σj∈S μj,k : S ⊆ Jk admitting an elementary route within L }

is a single-processor orienteering problem with item profits μj,k.

Proof of Proposition 1

Let (y,{Pk}) ∈ 𝒳. Each route Pk is elementary and within horizon L, hence feasible for the orienteering problem defining Ok(μ), so Σj∈Jk μj,k xj,k ≤ Ok(μ). For each job j, using μj,k ≥ 0 and yj ≤ xj,k,

bj yj = ( bj − Σk∈Kj μj,k ) yj + Σk∈Kj μj,k yj ≤ max( 0, bj − Σk∈Kj μj,k ) + Σk∈Kj μj,k xj,k

Summing over j and regrouping the last term by processor gives Σj bj yj ≤ Σj max(0, ·) + Σk Ok(μ) = L(μ). Maximising the left-hand side over 𝒳 yields z*SRPS ≤ L(μ). ∎

Both operations used — dualising (iii) with μ ≥ 0 and relaxing the synchronisation (ii) inside the subproblem — only enlarge the feasible region or add a non-negative penalty, so the bound is valid for every μ ≥ 0.

A.3 · Exact solution of Ok(μ) and its cost

Jobs with μj,k ≤ 0 never improve an orienteering objective and are removed a priori. Ok(μ) is then solved by bitmask dynamic programming: with dp[mask][i] the minimum time to serve exactly the jobs in mask and end at job i, the optimum is the largest profit Σj∈mask μj,k over masks whose best completion back to the depot is within L. This enumerates the optimal route over all subsets and is therefore exact: it uses no pruning, sampling, or candidate-set restriction. Its cost is O(2|Jk||Jk|²).

This is tractable at every benchmark scale because |Jk| is small and does not grow with n. Each job requires only |Kj|∈{2,3,4} of the |K|=55 processors, so the incidences Σk|Jk| = Σj|Kj| spread thinly: the per-instance mean |Jk| is 2.8–6.0, and the maximum over all 660 study instances is 13, attained already at n=80 and unchanged at n=150. Every subproblem therefore costs at most 213·13² ≈ 1.4×106 operations.

A.4 · Validity of the reported certificate

By Proposition 1 every iterate L(μt) is a valid upper bound, so mint L(μt) is valid; since all profits bj are integral we report UBlag = ⌊mint L(μt)⌋, which is also valid because no feasible objective lies strictly between the two, and the certified gap of Equation (10) is a rigorous bound on the suboptimality of the incumbent.

We report UBlag as the certificate, with one safeguard: a truncated subgradient run can in rare cases return an iterate numerically below a value already known to be a valid upper bound (for instance an independently proven optimum), and we never report the bound below such a value. This occurs on exactly one of the 660 instances (ED_n045_008_a75_024), where the recorded bound fell one profit unit below the BPC-proven optimum; reporting against the proven optimum yields a certified gap of 0.068% rather than 0%. The substitution can only enlarge a reported gap, never shrink it, so it is conservative and cannot create a falsely tight certificate.

Reproduction and results

All instruments are read-only with respect to the solver and modify no existing code path. The accompanying repository ships a single runner:

python reproduce.py --list          # every stage, its command, its runtime
python reproduce.py --all           # everything, about 6 hours
python reproduce.py --all --quick   # everything at reduced budgets
python reproduce.py --fragments     # rebuild this report's tables from the CSVs

Every table in Sections 9–10 is generated directly from the result files rather than transcribed. Artifacts are written to results/bpc_replica_dev/ (cut separation and its instruments), results/dual_guided_dev/ (coupling, seeding, stabilisation, fixing, parallel), and results/exact/ (the CPLEX reference). Result files are timestamped and never overwritten, so repeated runs accumulate.

The per-instance baseline results — incumbent, bound, certified gap, and runtime for all 780 instances — are in results/adaptive_master.csv. Every Δ reported here is measured against that table, so any arm can be recompared against the baseline directly.

The 71 instance files the experiments touch are bundled with the repository under CC0; the full benchmark is not needed to reproduce anything reported here. The benchmark instances are publicly available from Riera-Ledesma and Salazar-González [1] at github.com/RieraULL/OPS-Benchmark. The solver, experiment drivers, certificate verifier, and all result-generating scripts are in the submitted repository.

The single most common source of misreading is the integer-floor convention of Equation (10): a technique can improve the real-valued bound and change the certified gap by nothing. Repository documentation covers setup, the meaning of every result column, and this convention.
